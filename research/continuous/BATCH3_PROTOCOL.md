# Batch 3 protocols for label maturity and water allocation

## A3. Historical fixed-horizon outcome support

Audit Mission Assignments v2 for the full 689 postcoverage non-Biological major declarations already in the historical workbook. Restrict every financial request to explicit eligible FY2013–24 historical IDs and cap the collection at 60 pages. Keep authentic signed actions, source snapshots, counts and keys. Use accepted duplicate, date and amendment checks. Record missing public records separately from verified net zero. Define an audit quantity as the signed nominal dollars associated with actions dated through 36 calendar months after the declaration date. Report whether the snapshot is at least 90 days after that endpoint. Treat this as a fixed-horizon published-action quantity and leave public vintage and label completeness unresolved where not documented. Compare the 12-, 24-, 36-, 60-month and eventual snapshots only when their time window has elapsed. Count disagreements and threshold transitions without altering the original workbook.

Retain the exact forward origins and event purge. For funding diagnostics, count strictly prior 36-month plus 90-day support before the earlier of the first held incident or declaration. Require separately reported action coverage, known signed labels and event independence. Reevaluate the previous same-hazard/state support gates using standardized in-scope episode maxima. Additional target labels are an alternative endpoint and do not create new independent disaster episodes. Preserve the FY2025 lock. Consider a fixed-horizon prediction candidate only after this audit can define an interpretable training and evaluation population.

## B3. Two-day safe-water recourse simulation

The original seven-policy simulation evaluates aggregate service units under sequential budget increments. The new comparison adds explicit clean-water supply inventories, road throughput, day-specific demand and correlated day-two uncertainties. Use 15 litres per person per day as a documented reference service rate in the simulated contexts. Other needs, road capacity and vulnerability profiles are stylized assumptions. The study has no authentic municipal water deficits or operational response observations. [WHO emergency water guidance](https://www.who.int/teams/environment-climate-change-and-health/water-sanitation-and-health/environmental-health-in-emergencies/humanitarian-emergencies).

For each bounded scenario draw, simulate two supply hubs and six zones with initial stocks, observed day-one demand, and known initial route capacities. Supply restoration, demand from public-water disruption and road throughput on day two vary together in four possible states. Fix state probabilities before selecting a policy. Use the same realized demand, shipment feasibility, information chronology and recourse rules across policies. Safe-water deficits in day one count in the outcome even when day two has surplus stock.

Compare a vulnerability-weighted chronological myopic policy, equal-region preference as a diagnostic, expected weighted loss with two-stage linear recourse, and the same program adding 50% conditional-value-at-risk penalty for the upper quarter of losses. A single first-day shipment matrix applies across all future states. All policies see the actual state only for day-two dispatch. Count shipment and inventory constraint failures, total service fraction, higher-vulnerability service fraction and bottom-decile weighted service. A candidate policy is eligible for integration into the simulator only with at least a two-percentage-point paired improvement in bottom-decile weighted service relative to the vulnerability-weighted myopic reference and at most a one-point loss in mean total service, zero infeasibility and supportive episode-paired resampling. Compare at most 240 independent scenario draws. All conclusions apply to simulated service under the assumed constraints.
